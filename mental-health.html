Mental Health Inequalities in Times of Social Upheaval

Americans by age group and gender, 2002–2022

Authors. Leah Houseman, MA and Tuğce Kırmacı Kamber, MA.

Background. Mental health has become a barometer of societal well-being, shaping labour productivity, healthcare costs, and family stability. Persistent disparities across gender and age groups reveal how social structures, life-course transitions, and role expectations distribute psychological strain unevenly (Yu, 2018; Moen, 2022). Exogenous shocks — political, economic, and health crises — intensify that strain and have compounding effects on some groups. Women, for example, bore a disproportionate mental health burden during the COVID-19 pandemic (Arilha et al., 2024), while younger adults experience heightened anxiety tied to economic precarity and social instability (Vancea & Utzet, 2017).

Data & Methods. Using the American General Social Survey (2002–2022), we analysed trends in the average number of self-reported poor mental health days per month, by survey year. We stratified results by gender and age cohort, constructing time-series visualizations to identify both long-term trends and event-linked fluctuations around the 2008 financial crisis, the 2016 election, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Key findings. Three major patterns emerged. First, women at all times and across all ages report more poor mental health days than men. Second, major crises have a compounding effect on the gender gap in self-reported mental health days. Third, there is a sharp rise in reported poor mental health days for younger women (18–34) beginning in the late 2010s and intensifying with COVID-19.

Implications. These findings underscore the interplay between political-economic conditions and mental health. Structural shocks — whether economic recessions or heightened political polarization — reveal how uncertainty translates into individual psychological distress. Policymakers should view mental health not only as a clinical or individual concern but as a sensitive indicator of social stability and inequality. Without structural responses such as more equitable labour protections and gender-sensitive social policy, each new crisis risks deepening the uneven psychological toll of social uncertainty.
