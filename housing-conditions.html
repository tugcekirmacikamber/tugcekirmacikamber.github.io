Inequalities in Housing Conditions

Who lives in high-quality housing? Inequalities across intersected social groups

Authors. Valeriia Yakushko, Beren Crim Sabuncu, Tuğce Kırmacı Kamber, and Jiawen Li (Group 5).

Background. Housing risk is often hidden. Access to quality housing is stratified by race, immigrant status, and other social locations (Hulchanski, 2007; Swope & Hernandez, 2019), and the most invisible risk lies at the intersection of these locations. Family characteristics matter too: marital status and parenthood have become increasingly consequential indicators of social inequality (McLanahan & Percheski, 2008), shaping household income, economic mobility, and family formation. Housing quality — encompassing both housing condition and affordability (OECD, 2021) — has wide-ranging effects on health (Chapman et al., 2023), wellbeing (Riva et al., 2022; Clapham, 2018), and economic mobility (Ramakrishnan, 2021).

Findings. Drawing on the Socioeconomic–Mortality Linked Synthetic Dataset (February 2021) and binary logistic regression — restricted to individuals aged 18–65 and controlling for income, education, and minority status — we found two striking patterns. About 2 in 5 single men live in a dwelling needing repair, and nearly 2 in 5 single parents do as well. Being a single man, or being single with children, are strong predictors of living in a dwelling that needs repair.

Policy implications. For single-parent households, we recommend prioritizing direct repair funding for urgent health and safety risks and providing hands-on navigation support to ensure repairs actually happen. For single men, we recommend expanding rapid, low-barrier repair assistance (no credit checks, minimal paperwork) and enforcing landlord accountability in substandard rental housing. The broader takeaway: housing policy must move beyond access toward maintenance equity — safe, livable housing is an ongoing right, not a one-time provision.
