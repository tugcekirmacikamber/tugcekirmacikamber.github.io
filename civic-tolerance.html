Research Presentation · CAnD3 Dragon's Den

From Consensus to Divergence

Generational shifts in civic tolerance · United States, 1972–2024

Background. Tolerance for the exercise of civil liberties — freedom of speech, for instance — is a democratic institution, often used to measure acceptance of pluralism. The U.S. is a special case among democracies in that acceptable speech is far less regulated (Boch, 2020). Conventional measures of political tolerance tend to assume that all forms of speech are equally legitimate (or illegitimate), which would lead us to expect broadly rising tolerance toward both far-left and far-right ideologies. Yet a third category of tolerance is on the rise, termed multicultural tolerance (Harell, 2010), which prioritizes norms of equality over norms of freedom and differentiates speech based on its potential harm to historically marginalized communities, including BIPOC communities and sexual minorities (Chong, Citrin & Levy, 2024). Following Chong, Citrin and Levy (2024), I tracked public attitudes toward civil liberties in the U.S. to test whether this argument holds across generations.

Data & Methods. The U.S. General Social Survey (1972–2024) includes a civil-liberties battery asking respondents whether they would allow members of politically fringe groups to give a public speech, to teach in a school, and to have a book in the public library. I focus on tolerance towards groups at the fringes of conventional politics US, which are communists, racists, and militarists. Using logistic regression, I estimate the predicted probability of tolerance toward each group across these three questions, net of race, gender, education, and political affiliation.

Results. The first graph shows the changing windows of tolerance toward communists, racists, and militarists. From the Greatest Generation to the Baby Boomers, overall tolerance rises but shows little divergence between groups. The second graph tells the story of divergence: while tolerance toward militarists and communists stays high, tolerance of racists is markedly lower among younger generations. Gen Z shows the lowest tolerance toward racists, supporting the thesis that politically fringe ideals are now filtered through the lens of multicultural tolerance. Still, it is important to see that tolerance for racists declines sharply across generations with 2010s.

Implications. Cohort replacement may signal future shifts in norms and in divergent support for pro-civil-liberties policies. Gibson (2013) finds that such support is itself divided: strong rejection of racial profiling and strong backing for free protest coexist with strong support for surveillance when deemed necessary (wiretapping, email monitoring) and for requiring teachers to promote loyalty to the U.S. Future work should map the bounds of this selective approach among younger cohorts and how it shapes support for different civil-liberties policies.
